Chapter 1

The Engine

Pause, reset, strategy, plan. Email, FileMaker, accounting and the reports are one machine at an inflection point.

The Engine

Two systems, the people, the output, the books — one machine

Email

Every instruction, nomination, SOF and B/L arrives and leaves here. The shared office mailboxes are the operations record.

FileMaker

Port calls, vessels, cargo, disbursements, filings, CRM — everything except the ledger.

Reports

Lineups, export reports, summaries, the portal — assembled from FileMaker, sent by email, free to the customer.

Customers

Principals, elevators, traders and charterers — who pay for agency service and read what we send.

Accounting

Sage Intacct — fed from FileMaker by API. Disbursements, invoices, the money. Moved off FileMaker already, and it worked.

Operations

The port operations teams in every office, working the Pilotsheet all day — the core service, run inside FileMaker from nomination to sailing.

Outside relationships and the skill of the agents on the dock, this is how the company shows what it knows. Agency is people and relationships; the engine is how those people work and what they hand the customer. The reports, free to the customer, are the shop window — the visible proof of experience. Built in-house, it works, and it was an advantage. The question is whether it still is, and what it costs.
One machine, not three projects

Why working on them in silos has not worked

·
Change email
…and the shared operations mailboxes, the report distribution list, the attachments FileMaker links to, and the archive the P&I club will one day ask for all change with it.
·
Change FileMaker
…and every report's source data, the Intacct feed, the sailing-message templates that go out by email, and the customer portal change with it.
·
Change the reports
…and the data they need from FileMaker, the delivery path through email, and what the customer expects to receive change with it.

What is happening today

Four folders, four board lanes, three separate assessments, one person. Silos — and inside the FileMaker silo, sub-silos: a feature request, a data fix and a plugin upgrade all "in progress" at once. Piecemeal, each piece reasonable, and the person carrying it and the wider organisation cannot sustain it.

What follows from it

There cannot be an email decision, a FileMaker decision, an accounting decision and a reports decision. There is one decision about the engine, taken at a higher altitude with all of it in view — then the ground-level targets can be struck tactically and efficiently. That is why this deck asks for a pause rather than a platform.

Why piecemeal is expensive

The mechanics, from the two cases and the corpus

No single place holds a matter

Seen: Five subject lines for one sheet of plywood; eight threads for one FileMaker intent; a facilities item finishing inside a development thread.

Cost: Each hop drops context — seven questions died with a forked thread — and no subject line retrieves the matter afterwards.

Nobody holds the next step

Seen: Three silences of 2 months, 3½ weeks and 3 weeks; two ownership vacuums; 'they are waiting on you' / 'who is they?'

Cost: Elapsed time is the cost. 160 days for a morning's work; 26 days with a blocker's answer sitting unread in the mail.

The list and the queue are different things

Seen: The two hardest 'drop everything' interrupts were not on the numbered priority list; a platform migration was added, then parked in 13 days.

Cost: Capacity planned from the list understates the load. A two-hour task interrupted four times takes a day.

Closure by assertion

Seen: A board card reads 'done' against work not done; one "Yes" closes two jobs below their written scope; a backfill of ~40 records is flagged and never run.

Cost: Rework arrives later, unbudgeted, wearing a new subject line — and the reports built on the data inherit the gap.

Decisions leave no record

Seen: At least four hand-offs to the phone in one case; a revert proposal never withdrawn; sheets three to five weeks stale.

Cost: The next person — or the same person a month later — starts from zero. Key-person risk, manufactured daily.

The queue is a workshop, not a help desk

Seen: Faults are 5.5% of messages; development, reporting and programme work are over half; a third of help-desk threads need no IT judgement.

Cost: Running a workshop through a help-desk inbox means every build is an interrupt and every interrupt looks like a build.

None of this is effort or ability. It is the absence of an operating model — one door, one owner per matter, exit conditions, written decisions, verified closure. That is what the reset installs.
Evidence: the classified corpus (154 threads) and the two case reconstructions. Time figures are elapsed days from the record, never estimated minutes.

The threat

What we send for free, the market now sends better

What is changing outside the building

  • USDA now publishes interactive grain transportation and export dashboards — weekly, public, free, and better formatted than what we send
  • Commercial data vendors sell vessel tracking, port lineups and manifest data by subscription to anyone
  • Customers have analysts — traders and elevators increasingly build their own views from the same public feeds
  • Assembly is no longer the skill — software that compiles a lineup from a feed in seconds is now ordinary

What it means for the engine

  • A free report that repeats public data stops demonstrating anything the day the public version is better — and that day is close
  • What survives is what only an agent on the dock knows: the berth, the wait, the survey result, the reason for the delay, the relationship
  • The engine is optimised for the first kind of report and has no way to capture or deliver the second
  • So each hour spent maintaining it as-is buys less than the hour before — the definition of diminishing returns
The value these systems added was real and it is diminishing quickly. Not because they broke — because the world caught up. The pace of the technology and the administrative burden of producing the output make this an inflection point, not a maintenance problem.
An inflection point

The service is not in question — everything around it is

The whole scope is one realisation, not three problems

The core service — agency — is people, relationships and skill on the dock, and none of that is in question. Everything around it is: the presentation of the output, the tools the people work in, and the administrative burden of producing what the customer sees. The pace of the technology has made the gap visible. That is the inflection point.

Increase the value added

Output presented in the form the customer's business now runs on — timely, readable on a phone, carrying what only our agents know — so the free report keeps demonstrating experience.

Attract new business

The shop window is the first thing a prospective principal judges. A modern one wins nominations before anyone has met; a dated one loses them the same way.

Reduce the administrative burden

Every hour spent assembling a lineup by hand, hunting a mail attachment or fixing a laptop is an hour not spent on the dock or with a customer — and in a market that sets the price, it is margin.

Three outcomes, one programme. Any direction the working days choose is measured against these three — and the third one is the one the market lets us control.
Red ocean, purple water

When the market sets the price, you control three things

Red Ocean

Where agency lives
  • The market sets the price. Nobody in this trade names their own
  • An ancient trade, run on customs and practices the whole world accepts — the same core service from every competitor
  • Differentiating on price is a race to the bottom; differentiating on service is table stakes

Purple Water

Where Blue Water can move
  • Not a new market — a visibly different presence in the old one
  • Presentation and speed of output; information carrying what only our agents know; a customer experience that looks like this decade
  • Reached by taking cost and administration out of the engine and putting the time saved in front of the customer

Blue Ocean

Rare — worth a look, not a bet
  • A product nobody sells: our dock-level data combined with the public feeds, delivered as a service rather than an attachment
  • Block 2 asks whether it exists; block 4 scores it. Nothing in this deck depends on finding it
  • If it exists, it is built on the same modernised engine — which is why the pause comes first
Cost, cost and cost. When the price is set for you, margin is the only variable, and administration is where it leaks. Modernisation is a cost play first and a presentation play second — and the two are the same programme.
Red and blue ocean are the standard strategy terms; purple is ours — differentiated inside a market that sets the price.
Diminishing returns

Value peaked around 2016; we are in negative returns

Reading the curve

  • Phase 1: built in-house by people who knew the trade, every screen and report moved the company forward. That was real, and it lasted a decade.
  • Phase 2: additions kept it level while the outside world — public data, cheaper software, customers with analysts — caught up.
  • Phase 3: since about 2019 more input has produced less output: the hours to make the reports, the laptops to hold the mail, the one person who can change anything.
  • The audit's 200–400 developer-days holds the line — it stops the fall. It does not climb. Only the green curve climbs, and it starts from where we stand, not from the peak.
Illustrative curve, not measured data. The 2016 peak and the phase boundaries are the project manager's estimate; block 3 replaces them with the company's own cost and return figures.
What no assessment has answered

The business questions — and the metrics that answer them

·
What does the engine cost, all in — per port call?
5-year total cost of ownership: licences, hardware, the person, the contractor, the laptops bought for mail, the hours lost — divided by the calls it serves. TCO
·
What do the reports cost to produce — and what do they win?
Hours per issue across every office, against the retention and new business they visibly support. Cost-to-serve
·
What does a day down cost?
3 August: internet, email and FileMaker off together; staff sent home. Multiply by the number of such days. Cost of downtime
·
What is a lost decade of records worth?
One server, one building, no proven restore, no off-site copy, hurricane season. Revenue at risk
·
What would a pivot cost, and when does it pay back?
The green curve, priced: people, data, delivery — against the blue curve, priced the same way. Payback period
·
What do customers value that only we can give?
Not a survey — five conversations with principals and elevators about what they read, what they would miss, and what would win the next nomination. Value to customer
These are business questions, not IT questions — which is why IT cannot answer them alone, and why the working days need finance, strategy and operations in the room.

The proposal

Pause · Reset · Strategy · Plan

Pause
Now

Stop discretionary spend and feature work on every part of the engine. Keep only safety work and day-to-day support. Nothing new starts until the plan is signed.

Reset
Early October

One programme, one board, one folder, one owner. All the material — three assessments, the board, the reports, the numbers — in one pack. Customer conversations and the TCO build happen here, before anyone sits down.

Strategy
Mid October · 3 days

A mini team — strategy, finance, project management — spends a few days together and works through all of it: the engine as-is, the market, the economics, the options. SWOT, TCO, vendor evaluations, weighted scoring.

Plan
November

One consolidated plan with timelines and budgets, presented to ownership. Ownership agrees, adjusts or redirects; sign-off by mid-November; execution from then, on whichever curve was chosen.

Eight weeks. The cost of the pause is two months of features nobody has asked for. The cost of not pausing is another year of piecemeal work on the flat part of the curve, and a platform decision taken by default.
The pause

What stops, and what does not

Stops
  • New FileMaker features, layouts and dashboards — the request queue is frozen, not deleted
  • Plugin and version upgrades that are not security fixes
  • Laptops, drives and storage bought to hold email
  • The website rebuild and the Laravel migration
  • New software subscriptions — password manager, mail client platforms, proposals: all move to the Decide queue for block 4
  • Network kit beyond emergency repair, until the platform answer says what each office needs
  • Any new assessment, audit or consultant engagement
Continues
  • The 1 October server: install, the first proven restore, the sandbox, an off-site copy — the audit's Phase 0, required under every future
  • Gateway from reject to quarantine, so real mail stops being lost silently
  • POP → IMAP cleanup and server-side noise filters — cost nothing, remove tickets
  • The three admin logins — mail server, gateway, FileMaker Server
  • Day-to-day support through one door, in a support window, three things in progress at most
  • Fixes for a stopped operation — always, through the sandbox once it exists
  • The reports keep going out exactly as they are; nothing changes for a customer during the pause
The line between the columns: does it make the company safer, or does it make the engine bigger? Safer continues. Bigger waits.
The reset

One programme, one board, one pack, one set of roles

One programme

The Engine programme, three pillars — email, FileMaker, reports — on one foundation of network and support. No pillar has its own plan; the programme has one.

One board

Four queues instead of one pile: Run (today's incidents) · Fix (weekly FileMaker batch) · Build (three active, max) · Decide (ownership, monthly). A label legend. Nothing enters except through the intake form.

One pack

All the material is now in one folder. Before the working days it becomes one reading pack: the three assessments' summaries, the issues log, the board, the report inventory, and the numbers we already hold.

One set of roles

Ownership decides platform, money and policy. The project manager owns sequence, calendar and the pack. IT executes and advises. Operations managers own what the reports must do. Accounting owns the numbers.

The consolidation of the folders is already done; the board re-lay and the reading pack are the first two weeks of October.

SWOT — starter draft

Drawn from what we already hold — block 4 tears it up

Strengths

  • Twenty years of agency expertise encoded in the data model — vessel, port call, principal, laycan, berth, cargo
  • A properly built CRM: 8,495 companies, 6,744 contacts, audit-logged
  • A decade of operating data — 507,551 export records — that nobody else holds
  • Customer relationships and the skill of the agents; the engine serves them, not the reverse
  • Accounting already migrated to Intacct by API — the engine can integrate when asked to

Weaknesses

  • One person able to maintain the engine; no build documentation; no version history
  • No proven restore; live file doubles as the development environment
  • Email on end-of-life hardware; POP scatters the only copy across 2 TB laptops; no archive or hold
  • Reports assembled by hand, sent as attachments, unchanged for years, unmeasured
  • No standards — every office network, every laptop, every request is bespoke

Opportunities

  • A modern suite lays the foundation in one move: files, archive, shared mailboxes, security
  • Own dock-level data plus public feeds → reports a customer cannot get from USDA or a vendor
  • A sandbox makes change safe for the first time — and makes a second developer possible
  • Purple water: in a market that sets the price, presentation and speed of output are the differentiators left
  • All the material is in one place; the company can, for the first time, describe its own engine

Threats

  • USDA's free interactive dashboards and commercial data vendors — our reports repeat what they publish
  • Wire-fraud email: a spoofed instruction is the loss that ends relationships
  • One server, one building, Gulf coast, no off-site copy — hurricane season every year
  • The one person leaves, or the contractor's start date arrives with no scope
  • The market sets the price — margin is cost, and every hour of administration is margin given away

The directions to score

Four directions — safety work first, then they diverge

A
Stabilise and modernise in place

Modern email suite; FileMaker rewired in place over 18–30 months; reports refreshed in format but not in kind.

Lowest risk. Stays on the blue curve. The audit's platform recommendation — and its honest limit.

B
Stabilise and pivot the reports

Same foundation, but the reports become a product: dock-level data the agents already capture, combined with public feeds, delivered on a platform rather than by attachment.

The green curve. Needs a product owner from operations and a data path out of FileMaker. Highest return if customers value it — block 2 tells us.

C
Stabilise and buy

Modern suite plus a commercial agency platform; FileMaker retired over time; reports from the vendor's tooling.

Not yet assessed by anyone — the audit says so. Real cost of migration and retraining ~60 staff. Block 3 prices it.

D
Stabilise and outsource the run

Managed service for email, network and desktop; in-house effort concentrated on FileMaker and the reports.

Directly addresses the one-person risk. Can combine with A or B. Cheapest to test — a quote.

Not a menu of four. The likely answer combines them — B on top of A's foundation, with D taking the run work. Block 4 scores each part, not each letter.
Scored on cost-to-serve first — the three C's — and on presentation second. The audit scored A–D for the platform alone; this widens the lens to the whole engine.

Chapter 2

Agency Platform

The port call as the pipeline. A concept for the port-call record the audit found missing: eight stages from nomination to settlement, with port operations, accounting and forwarding as parallel tracks.

Why a pipeline

Today, and what one record per port call changes

Today
  • Hundreds of emails a day per agent — orders, confirmations, updates, invoices, all in one inbox
  • The port call has no single identity — it lives in a FileMaker record, a mailbox, a spreadsheet and a head, and the audit measured the gap
  • Disbursement accounts built by hand — estimate, funding, actuals and settlement in separate places
  • Nothing connects — vessel tracking, email, accounting, documents are separate systems
  • Tribal knowledge — the critical detail is in someone's memory, not the record
  • No visibility for the principal — 'where's my DA?' is a phone call
One record
  • One port call, one status, one screen — every call by stage, port and vessel
  • Eight stages, three tracks — port operations, accounting and forwarding each with their own tasks, on the same record

Why the stages matter more than the screens. A port call with one governed status is what the FileMaker audit found the platform does not have: today a call is 'sailed' when a script moves it, 'billed' when accounting says so, and 'done' when the agent stops thinking about it. Eight named stages, each with an entry and exit condition, give every office, every report and every principal the same answer to 'where is this vessel?' — and every task, cost and document a place to hang.
Stages are the owner's own eight; the March concept used nine. Entry and exit conditions are for the operations managers to write.

Inside stage 4

In port is where the record earns its keep

At anchor

Waiting for berth. NOR tendered; laytime may start; anchorage costs accrue.

Berthed

All fast; CBP entry filed; draft survey; dockage begins; crew changes.

Working cargo

Loading or discharging; SOF building itself; delays logged (weather, breakdown); short-funding alerts.

Cargo complete

Final draft survey; SOF circulated for signature; outbound pilots ordered; departure prep.

Task checklist

Expense tracking

Every cost line: estimated → accrued → invoice received → verified → approved → paid.

Timeline / SOF

Events logged as they happen; the Statement of Facts assembles itself.

Funding monitor

Running balance against burn rate; alert before the call goes short-funded.

Following the money

The disbursement account is the backbone of every call

1
Proforma DAStages 1–3
Agent estimates all costs before arrival — pilotage, towage, dockage, stevedoring, agency fee — and sends it to the principal for approval.
2
Funding requestStages 2–4
Principal wires funds against the estimate. Every receipt tracked; running balance shown; short-funding flagged before it bites.
3
Live expense trackingStages 4–6
As vendor invoices arrive, each line moves: estimated → accrued → invoice received → verified → approved → paid.
4
Final DAStage 7
All invoices in; actuals compiled; proforma-to-actual variance shown; agency fee calculated; FDA rendered.
5
SettlementStage 8
Settlement = FDA less funds received. AR aged 30/60/90; reminders automatic; paid; the record locks.
Proforma accuracy becomes a number. Estimate-to-actual trending by port, vessel type and cargo — the audit's 'money trail' answered on every call, not once.
